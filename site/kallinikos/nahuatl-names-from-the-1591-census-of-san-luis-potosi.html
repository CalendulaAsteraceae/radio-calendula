Nahuatl Names from the 1591 Census of San Luis Potosi

By Kallinikos Gavras

Published 23 October 2026

In 1591, the Spanish official overseeing the mining town of San Luis Potosi in New Spain ordered a census of a group of Nahuatl-speaking Tlaxcalans (a group closely related to the Aztecs) who had been relocated to the town. Some men were recorded with fully Spanish names. Others had Spanish baptismal names as first names, and Nahuatl names taking the place of Spanish surnames. Most likely, these were the names that they or their parents used before converting. Women and children were only recorded by their first name.

This census is found in “Cuenta por sus nombres de los indios de Tlaxcala que vinieron a poblar entre los chichimecas.—1591” [“Account for the names of the indians of Tlaxcala who came to live among the chichimecas.—1591”]. This document was collected in the volume Coleccion de Documentos para la historia de San Luis Potosi [Collection of documents on the history of San Luis Potosi], compiled in 1897 by Feliciano Velasquez. The original document is held by the Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León, and can be accessed here and here.

Spanish names from the same Census can be found in the article Spanish Names from the 1591 Tlaxcala Census.

Some notes:

  • Nahuatl is completely without grammatical gender, so there is no way to tell from the structure of a name if it was used by a man or a woman. We also do not have strong evidence of completely separate naming pools, so these names could presumably have been used by someone of either gender
  • Where an entry says “he/she”, this should be understood as being equally applicable to someone regardless of gender.
  • While all names in this list were recorded as bynames [any sort of secondary name used for identification], they were all originally personal names that became bynames under Spanish rule.
  • Many of these names are spelled differently in standardized Classical Nahuatl orthography, but this standardization had not yet taken place, nor were the scribes consistent even in how they spelled the same names. Original orthography has been kept, save for fixing a few obvious spelling mistakes.
  • Some names were recorded without their nominitive suffix. Where the word has a clear nominitive suffix it has been included in the notes, even if names were commonly recorded both with and without suffix.
