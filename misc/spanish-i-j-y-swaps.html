Spanish I-J-Y Swaps: Evidence Tables and Frequency Analysis

By Měryem al Ixbilǐa
meryemsca@gmail.com

Published 25 September 2026

Introduction

In this article, I use data from CORDE, the Real Académica Española’s Corpus Diacrónico del Español, to provide evidence for swapping ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩, ⟨y⟩, ⟨g⟩, and ⟨x⟩ in pre-1600 Spanish. I show that ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩ and ⟨y⟩ may all represent the vowel /i/ and semivowel /j/ in any position of a word; that ⟨hi⟩, ⟨hj⟩, and ⟨hj⟩ may also represent word-initial /i/; and that ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩, ⟨g + e,i⟩, and ⟨x⟩ may all represent the consonants /d͡ʒ/ and /ʃ/ in any position of a word. Based on a hopefully-representative but unsystematically-chosen set of words, I also assess how common it is to use each letter to represent a sound in a particular position of a word.

For SCA name submissions, you can often use CORDE to directly document a spelling; note that it is case-sensitive and name elements might appear in uppercase or lowercase, with or without diacritics. Juliana de Luna has written instructions on how to search CORDE for a name.

According to Steven N. Dworkin’s A Guide to Old Spanish (2018), the following correspondences were common in Castilian Hispano-Romance by the thirteenth century: ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩ and ⟨y⟩ for the vowel /i/; ⟨y⟩ for the semivowel /j/; ⟨g + e,i⟩ and ⟨j⟩ for /d͡ʒ/; and ⟨x⟩ for /ʃ/.

Based on the data I collected from CORDE, for word-initial /i/, ⟨i⟩, ⟨y⟩, and ⟨hi⟩ are common while ⟨j⟩, ⟨hj⟩, and ⟨hy⟩ are rare. For word-medial /i/, ⟨i⟩ is common while ⟨j⟩ and ⟨y⟩ are rare. For word-final /i/, ⟨i⟩ and ⟨y⟩ are common while ⟨j⟩ is rare. For the semivowel /j/, ⟨y⟩ is common, ⟨i⟩ is used sometimes, and ⟨j⟩ is rare. For word-initial /d͡ʒ/ or /ʃ/, ⟨j⟩ is common, ⟨i⟩ is used sometimes, and ⟨g + e,i⟩ and ⟨x⟩ appear rare. For word-medial /d͡ʒ/ or /ʃ/, ⟨i⟩ and ⟨j⟩ are common while ⟨g + e,i⟩ and ⟨x⟩ appear rare. (The data for /d͡ʒ/ and /ʃ/ might be an artifact of the sampling procedure, and is not the focus of this article.)

For the word “and”, ⟨y⟩ is found from the 13th century onwards, ⟨i⟩ from c. 1180 onwards, ⟨j⟩ in the 13th and 14th centuries, and ⟨e⟩ and ⟨et⟩ throughout period.

Ways to say and

Historical spellings of “and” in Spanish include ⟨y⟩, ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩, ⟨e⟩, ⟨et⟩ and ⟨&⟩. Given the difficulty of searching CORDE for this word without false positives, I have not attempted to provide any frequency data. However, I did check whether the spellings of interest were used in each 50-year time period (until 1100, 1101 to 1150, 1151 to 1200, etc.). Documents of uncertain date were included in all possible 50-year periods.

  • ⟨y⟩ is very difficult to search for in CORDE because it’s interpreted as an OR operator. I have not found it as a spelling of the conjunction “and” earlier than the 1201–1250 window, but that doesn’t mean it definitely can’t be found.
  • ⟨i⟩ first appears as a spelling of the conjunction “and” in Auto de los Reyes Magos (c. 1180), e.g. “en pace i en guera”, and is found in every subsequent 50-year period.
  • ⟨j⟩ is occasionally found as a spelling of the conjunction “and”. It first appears in Fuero de las Quintanillas, e.g. “en Castiela j en Toledo” (1219), last appears in Tratado de Cetrería, e.g. “mas dulçes j de mayor talento” (c. 1350–1400), and is found in every 50-year period in between.
  • ⟨e⟩ is found as a spelling of the conjunction “and” in every 50-year period, both before /i/ (e.g. e hijos) and before other sounds.
  • ⟨et⟩ is found as a spelling of the conjunction “and”, in a Spanish context, in every 50-year period.
  • ⟨&⟩ is found as a spelling of the conjunction “and” in every 50-year period. (As a scribal abbreviation, it is not registrable in SCA names.)
  • I have not found any instances of ⟨hi⟩ or ⟨hi⟩ as a spelling of the conjunction “and”, but I did not look very thoroughly so I might have missed some.

In Old Spanish, ⟨y⟩ was also an adverbial pronoun meaning “there”. In Dworkin’s edition of the General estoria, cuarta parte (c. 1280), it appears in the phrase “nin posava y nuncua ninguno” (page 107). The pronoun is also found in CORDE spelled as ⟨i⟩, e.g. “et iuret que i las tomo” (1179–1184); ⟨j⟩, e.g. “fueron puestos indevidamente j el término común de la dicha çibdat e de su tierra” (1415); ⟨hi⟩, e.g. “quanto hi a nos pertenez” (1229); and ⟨hy⟩, e.g. “pornas hy almoianegues” (c. 1200).

Methodology

I started with CORDE’s frequency list of how often each spelling variant appears in the dataset, in total and in a 50-year time period (until 1200, 1201 to 1250, 1251 to 1300, etc.). Next, I cleaned up the formatting, removed post-1600 data, added transcriptions of the period forms without diacritics to make the data easier to filter, and filtered for words which contained ⟨i⟩, ⟨j⟩ or ⟨y⟩ with or without diacritics. I then made a list of representative words, filtered for data about those words specifically, and analyzed how frequently each letter was used in various positions for /i/, /j/, and /d͡ʒ/ or /ʃ/. The words I chose are reasonably common, but the percentages might not be representative of the entire corpus. However, I think they give some sense of the relative frequencies of different spellings.

It’s important to note that we can’t naïvely add frequencies from different time frames to get the total occurrences before 1600, because many documents are of uncertain date and the frequency list includes them in all the possible 50-year periods. If we add up the counts across all time spans, it will sometimes be greater than the total count.

The dataset has a few other limitations. It includes debatable words like Roman numerals, including ones like vi that are also normal words, so those particular numbers are less enlightening than might be hoped. It also includes words in Latin, which was often mixed in with Spanish documents in period, and probably other languages. I have not included words where this is a concern in the evidence tables.

My data and code is available on GitHub, and the charts were created by importing the data to Google Sheets.
