Introduction

When researching Andalusi Arabic for my own SCA name, I discovered a romanization system developed in 16th-century Spain, which was based on and accurately reflected the pronunciation of Andalusi Arabic. After doing the research to register my name with the College of Arms, I’d like to write it up properly so you can use it too, and so I can write shorter documentation in the future.

Section PN2D of the Standards for Evaluation of Names and Armory (SENA) requires that names not written in the Latin alphabet be transcribed in Latin letters. So far, all the systems we’ve used for Arabic that are noted in Appendix D (Hans Wehr, American Library Association/Library of Congress, and Encyclopaedia of Islam) are modern systems tailored to Literary Arabic. In this article, I will use the Encyclopaedia of Islam 3 (EI3) system as a point of comparison.

Due to the influence of Andalusi Romance, Andalusi Arabic used vowel stress in place of the long vowels of Literary Arabic. Andalusi Arabic also had extensive imāla (fronting and raising of /aː/ toward /iː/ or /eː/, and /a/ toward /i/ or /e/). For more information on the phonology of Andalusi Arabic, see A Descriptive and Comparative Grammar of Andalusi Arabic (2013).

A note on orthography: the works which form the basis of this article were published in 1505 and 1566. Capitalization and spacing are inconsistent in the 1505 work (or follow rules I’m not aware of). I have normalized capitalization, spacing, and <i>/<j> and <u>/<v> swaps but not <i>/<y> swaps, and added emphasis markings where they were omitted; otherwise, I have left the original orthography intact. For more information on <i>/<y> swaps in Early Modern Spanish, see my article on Spanish I-J-Y swaps [FIXME link to published article].
